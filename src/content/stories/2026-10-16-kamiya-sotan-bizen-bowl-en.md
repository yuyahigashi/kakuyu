Bizen pottery remains a living Japanese craft. Its history survives not only in vessels and kiln sites, but also in words describing how people used it.

One such trace appears in the sixteenth-century tea record known as the *Sōtan Nikki*. Its reference to a Bizen bowl concerns neither a celebrated name nor an impressive price. It describes food being served.

## A Merchant and Tea Practitioner from Hakata

Kamiya Sōtan (1553–1635) was a merchant and tea practitioner active in Hakata during the Azuchi–Momoyama and early Edo periods. His surname appears in different written forms in Japanese. Commerce and tea were not separate worlds in his life.

According to Shogakukan's biographical account, he helped with Hakata's reconstruction under Toyotomi Hideyoshi and associated with Sen no Rikyū and Tsuda Sōgyū. Hideyoshi invited him to tea gatherings; he later remained an influential merchant under the Kuroda family.

Fukuoka City Museum also places Sōtan within the networks of merchant tea practitioners in the trading port of Hakata. Tea was more than an interest in beautiful objects: it was a setting for encounters between people. [Fukuoka City Museum, Tea Culture in Hakata](https://museum.city.fukuoka.jp/archives/leaflet/159/index.html)

An important distinction follows. Recording a vessel at a gathering does not establish that Sōtan owned it.

## What the Diary Preserves

The *Sōtan Nikki* records gatherings with which Sōtan was involved. Shogakukan dates its coverage from late November 1586 to December 1613 and identifies it as one of the four major tea records. It describes practitioners, the forms and purchase prices of utensils, and aspects of social life.

Once a gathering ends, its arrangement and food disappear. A written record allows later readers to consider what was displayed, what was used, and how guests were received. Words can preserve something an object alone cannot: a situation in which it was used.

## A Bizen Bowl in 1594

Shogakukan's *Nihon Kokugo Daijiten*, under the entry for *Bizen-bachi*, cites a passage dated the seventh day of the fourth month of Bunroku 3 (1594). In our translation, it reads:

> Large sasa-wrapped chimaki were served in a Bizen bowl, immersed in hot water.

*Chimaki* are leaf-wrapped preparations associated with rice; the passage does not provide a recipe. The date is expressed in the historical Japanese calendar, not as April 7 in the modern Gregorian calendar.

The significant action is serving. The bowl appears as a vessel for food, not merely as an object on display. Looking at tea history only through drinking bowls would miss this part of the occasion.

The limits of the excerpt matter too. It gives no measurements, maker, or description of the fired surface. The adjective “large” describes the chimaki and does not establish that the bowl itself was enormous. Nor does this sentence alone identify its owner or the person serving. We should not turn it into an invented scene of Hideyoshi or Sōtan personally presenting the dish.

## Does “Bizen Bowl” Mean a Grinding Bowl?

A *hachi* is an open vessel deeper than a plate. Shogakukan defines *Bizen-bachi* as a Bizen pottery bowl, a term that can also refer to a Bizen grinding bowl, or *suribachi*.

That range of meanings does not prove the vessel in this entry was a grinding bowl. The excerpt mentions neither a grooved interior nor grinding food. What it establishes is a serving use for a vessel called a Bizen bowl.

Separating the name of an object from its use on a particular occasion makes the evidence clearer, not less interesting.

## Between Practical Pottery and Tea

Bizen's history includes the production of practical jars and grinding bowls. During the Momoyama period, a variety of forms also entered the world of tea, including flower vessels, water jars, bowls, and dishes. Mie Prefectural Art Museum describes this diversity in its account of Bizen pottery. [Mie Prefectural Art Museum, Bizen](https://www.bunka.pref.mie.lg.jp/art-museum/55573038646.htm)

The diary entry does not prove that an everyday object became “art” for the first time on this particular day. It tells us something more specific: a Bizen vessel was part of the act of serving food.

Does a vessel's significance reside entirely within the object? What is placed inside it, how it is offered, and with whom the moment is shared also shape the experience. Sōtan's record opens a way to consider pottery together with the actions surrounding it.

## A Name That Continued in Literature

Shogakukan also supplies later examples from *Kokon Buke Seisuiki* and the puppet-theater text *Ikudama Shinjū*. The former connects the vessel with breakage and caution; the latter uses it in a scene of a blow to the head.

These belong to different periods and literary contexts. They do not trace the subsequent history of the bowl mentioned in 1594. They do show that the name Bizen-bachi survived beyond a tea record. Literary scenes should not, however, be treated as direct evidence of ordinary behavior.

## Returning Time to the Object

An old Bizen vessel invites attention to its shape and surface. The diary adds food and human action to that view.

This article has not identified the actual bowl in the entry. Nevertheless, the words allow us to recognize pottery within the practices of daily life and hospitality. Sōtan preserved more than the name of a vessel: he left a brief fragment of the time in which it was used.
