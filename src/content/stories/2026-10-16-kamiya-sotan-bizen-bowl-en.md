Everyday vessels might be called one of our oldest languages.

Not a language of spoken words or writing, but one through which the shapes and uses of ordinary things convey something of earlier lives. Serving food. Holding water. Offering something to another person. A vessel has no voice, yet it can preserve traces of human time.

Bizen pottery is a traditional Japanese craft still practiced today. Many lives intersect with its long history, and we can still use Bizen vessels in our own daily routines. Our circumstances differ from those of earlier centuries, but putting food in a bowl and sharing a meal remain recognizable acts. That common ground can bring distant people a little closer.

One of them is Kamiya Sōtan, a merchant and practitioner of tea. His record of tea gatherings, the *Sōtan Nikki*, contains a brief reference to a Bizen vessel.

## Chimaki Served in a Bizen Bowl

Shogakukan's dictionary cites the following example from the entry for the seventh day of the fourth month of Bunroku 3 (1594). In our translation:

> Large sasa-wrapped chimaki were served in a Bizen bowl, immersed in hot water.

The date follows the calendar used at the time; it is not April 7 in the modern Gregorian calendar.

This is a record of an object in use: what was put inside it and how it was served. A few words place Bizen pottery within an act of hospitality.

But what exactly is a Bizen bowl?

## A Bowl: A Vessel for Food and Water

A *hachi*, or bowl, is deeper than a plate, with a mouth wider than a bottle's and a more open form than a narrow-mouthed jar. Think of a large serving bowl or a deep container for food. Such vessels can be made from wood, stone, or metal as well as ceramics.

*Bizen-bachi* means a bowl made in Bizen ware. Shogakukan's definition also includes a Bizen grinding bowl, or *suribachi*: a vessel used to crush or grind ingredients.

That range of meanings does not prove that the bowl in Sōtan's record was a grinding bowl. The excerpt offers no detailed description of its interior or shape. What it establishes is that a vessel called a Bizen bowl was used to serve chimaki, a leaf-wrapped food.

Likewise, “large” describes the chimaki, not the dimensions of the bowl. The sentence cannot reconstruct the object's entire appearance, but it does preserve something of its use.

## Who Was Kamiya Sōtan?

Kamiya Sōtan (1553–1635) was a prominent merchant in Hakata, Kyushu, during the Azuchi–Momoyama and early Edo periods. His surname appears in more than one written form in Japanese.

Born into a trading family, he experienced the destruction of Hakata in warfare and moved with his father to Karatsu. When Toyotomi Hideyoshi later pursued Hakata's reconstruction, Sōtan became involved alongside Shimai Sōshitsu and others. His work extended beyond buying and selling to rebuilding a town.

He was also deeply involved in tea. He associated with Sen no Rikyū and Tsuda Sōgyū, and Hideyoshi invited him to tea gatherings. Fukuoka City Museum describes the connections linking Hakata's merchant community with powerful figures and leading tea practitioners. [Fukuoka City Museum, Tea Culture in Hakata](https://museum.city.fukuoka.jp/archives/leaflet/159/index.html)

His activities were not confined to culture. According to Shogakukan's biographical account, he helped procure and transport military supplies at Nagoya in Kyushu during Hideyoshi's invasions of Korea. After the Battle of Sekigahara, he served as a merchant supplying the Kuroda family.

Knowing Sōtan means encountering more than a tea enthusiast. It means encountering a period in which trade, politics, warfare, and culture overlapped.

## What Did the Sōtan Nikki Record?

The *Sōtan Nikki* documents tea gatherings in which Sōtan was involved. Shogakukan dates its coverage from late November 1586 to December 1613 and identifies it as one of the four major tea records.

A tea record preserves who met, which utensils were used, and what took place. Sōtan recorded the activities of tea practitioners, the shapes of objects, and their purchase prices. His attention also extended to the people around him and the society in which they lived.

The passages concerning Hideyoshi are particularly significant. Records of gatherings at Osaka Castle in 1587, for example, help readers consider the settings Hideyoshi created and his relationships with others. An account of tea can therefore also become evidence for political and social history.

Alongside that larger history survives a small action: serving chimaki in a Bizen bowl. It supplies a concrete detail of the occasion that the names of powerful people alone cannot convey.

Recording a vessel does not establish that Sōtan owned it. This particular excerpt does not identify either the bowl's owner or the person who served the food.

## Meeting Earlier Lives Through Objects

The name Bizen-bachi also appears in later writing. Shogakukan supplies examples from *Kokon Buke Seisuiki* and the puppet-theater text *Ikudama Shinjū*. The former connects it with breakage and caution; the latter places it in a scene of a blow to the head. The vessel's name thus survives outside the setting of tea, although literary scenes cannot simply be treated as evidence of everyday habits.

Objects change in use and meaning over time. Yet holding a vessel, filling it with food, or occasionally breaking it remains an experience we can recognize.

This article has not identified the actual bowl described in the *Sōtan Nikki*. Nevertheless, when we hold Bizen pottery today, that passage can turn our attention toward someone using a vessel centuries ago.

If everyday objects are a language, it is not one that explains everything about the past. It is a language through which our own lives give us a starting point for asking questions of earlier ones.
